Non-Stationarity: Why Raw Prices Break ML Models

📉 Raw Price (Non-Stationary)

BAD FOR STATS

Mean drifts upwards, variance expands. Correlating two random stocks yields fake 0.90 correlations.

Mean(t) ≠ Mean(t+k)  | Variance → ∞

📈 Log Returns (Stationary)

QUANT NATIVE

ln(P_t / P_{t-1}) oscillates cleanly around zero. Statistically normal-ish, additive, and symmetrical.

Symmetric: +10% then -10% = Net 0.00

Visual Knowledge Checkpoint
1. Why does label="right" secretly leak future prices into the past? ▼
Setting label="right" timestamps the bar using the end boundary (09:16) for ticks collected between 09:15:00 and 09:15:59. Your backtest code at 09:15 mistakenly thinks this completed data was already known, causing lookahead bias.
2. Why are Log Returns additive over time, while Simple Returns are not? ▼
Because ln(P2/P0) = ln(P2/P1) + ln(P1/P0). You can calculate a 5-minute return by simply summing five 1-minute log returns. Simple returns require compounding multiplication: (1+r1)*(1+r2) - 1.
3. Why must live orders ALWAYS use unadjusted prices? ▼
Adjusted prices are mathematical fictions created for continuous backtesting. The real NSE matching engine only understands raw Last Traded Price (LTP). Sending an order at the back-adjusted ₹15,000 for a stock trading at ₹150,000 will be instantly rejected.
4. What is the danger of using ffill() on missing bars in illiquid stocks? ▼
ffill() fabricates synthetic candles with identical Open, High, Low, and Close. An algorithm interprets this zero-movement candle as "ultra-low volatility stability," hiding the fact that zero buyers existed in the market!
5. Why do Dollar Bars have better statistical properties than Time Bars? ▼
Dollar bars slice data every time ₹N turnover transacts. They accelerate bar generation during heavy institutional trading bursts and slow down during dead midday lunch lulls. The resulting returns are significantly closer to a normal Gaussian distribution.
